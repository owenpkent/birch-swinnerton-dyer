# Reading note: M. Bhargava, A. Shankar, "Binary quartic forms having bounded invariants, and the boundedness of the average rank of elliptic curves," Annals of Mathematics 181 (2015), 191-242. arXiv:1006.1002.

> **Role in the BSD program.** This is the foundational paper of the statistics-of-Selmer architecture (architecture 5 in the atlas). It proves that the average size of the $2$-Selmer group $S_2(E)$ over all $E/\mathbb{Q}$, ordered by height, is exactly $3$. Since the $2$-Selmer rank bounds the Mordell-Weil rank, this bounds the average Mordell-Weil rank above by $1.5$, the first finite (unconditional) bound on the average rank. It is the engine behind Bhargava-Skinner-Zhang (positive proportion satisfy BSD), so it sits one level below the per-curve BSD statement.

## One-line takeaway

When all elliptic curves $E/\mathbb{Q}$ are ordered by naive height $H(E_{A,B}) = \max\{4|A|^3, 27B^2\}$, the average size of the $2$-Selmer group is exactly $3$ (Theorem 1.1), proved unconditionally by counting $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$-orbits of integral binary quartic forms of bounded invariants via geometry of numbers; this forces the average $2$-Selmer rank below $1.5$ and so the average Mordell-Weil rank below $1.5$, but it is a statement about the family and certifies nothing about the rank, finiteness of Sha, or BSD for any individual curve, in particular nothing in the open rank $\geq 2$ regime.

## Technical content (section by section)

**Section 1.1: the main theorem and what it buys.** Every $E/\mathbb{Q}$ is written uniquely as $E_{A,B}: y^2 = x^3 + Ax + B$ with $A, B \in \mathbb{Z}$ minimal ($p^6 \nmid B$ whenever $p^4 \mid A$), height $H(E_{A,B}) = \max\{4|A|^3, 27B^2\}$. The $2$-Selmer group sits in the exact sequence
$$0 \to E(\mathbb{Q})/2E(\mathbb{Q}) \to S_2(E) \to \mathrm{Sha}_E[2] \to 0, \tag{1}$$
an elementary abelian $2$-group of order $2^s$; $s$ is the $2$-Selmer rank.

> **Theorem 1.1.** When all elliptic curves $E/\mathbb{Q}$ are ordered by height, the average size of the $2$-Selmer group $S_2(E)$ is $3$.

> **Corollary 1.2.** Their average $2$-Selmer rank is at most $1.5$; thus their average rank is at most $1.5$.

The logic of the corollary is exact and worth recording, because it is where the inequality (not equality) enters. Equation (1) gives
$$r_2(S_2(E)) = r(E) + r_2(E(\mathbb{Q})[2]) + r_2(\mathrm{Sha}_E[2]), \tag{2}$$
where $r_2$ is $\mathbb{F}_2$-dimension and $r(E)$ is the Mordell-Weil rank. From $2 r_2(S_2(E)) \le 2^{r_2(S_2(E))} = |S_2(E)|$, Theorem 1.1 bounds the mean of the left side of (2) by $3/2$. This is a one-directional bound: the average rank could be much smaller (Goldfeld's minimalist conjecture predicts $1/2$, which remains a conjecture and is not proved here), and the bound says nothing about the maximum rank.

Prior bounds on average rank were all conditional: Brumer (ref [11]) got $\le 2.3$ under GRH + BSD; Heath-Brown $\le 2$; Young $\le 25/14 \approx 1.79$. Theorem 1.1 is the first unconditional finiteness.

> **Theorem 1.3.** When elliptic curves $E: y^2 = x^3 + Ax + B$ in any family defined by finitely many congruence conditions on $A, B$ are ordered by height, the average size of $S_2(E)$ is $3$.

This stronger version (the average is robust to congruence subfamilies, and in fact holds for certain infinite sets of congruence conditions) is what the elliptic-curve application actually consumes.

**Section 1.2: the counting backbone (binary quartic forms).** The arithmetic engine is a class-number asymptotic for binary quartic forms, extending Gauss (quadratic, ref Theorem 1.4, Mertens 1874 / Siegel 1944) and Davenport (cubic, ref Theorem 1.5, 1951). A binary quartic $f(x,y) = ax^4 + bx^3y + cx^2y^2 + dxy^3 + ey^4$ has two fundamental $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$-invariants
$$I(f) = 12ae - 3bd + c^2, \qquad J(f) = 72ace + 9bcd - 27ad^2 - 27eb^2 - 2c^3,$$
with discriminant $\Delta(f) = (4 I(f)^3 - J(f)^2)/27$ and height $H(I,J) = \max\{|I|^3, J^2/4\}$.

> **Theorem 1.6.** Let $h^{(i)}(I,J)$ count $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$-classes of irreducible integral binary quartic forms with $4 - 2i$ real roots in $\mathbb{P}^1$ and invariants $I, J$. Then, summing over $H(I,J) < X$,
> $$\sum h^{(0)} = \tfrac{4}{135}\zeta(2) X^{5/6} + O(X^{3/4+\epsilon}), \quad \sum h^{(1)} = \tfrac{32}{135}\zeta(2) X^{5/6} + O(X^{3/4+\epsilon}), \quad \sum h^{(2)} = \tfrac{8}{135}\zeta(2) X^{5/6} + O(X^{3/4+\epsilon}).$$

Theorem 1.7 characterizes which pairs $(I,J)$ are "eligible" (occur as invariants of some integral form) by congruences mod $27$. Theorem 1.8 packages 1.6 and 1.7 into the statement that the number of classes per eligible $(I,J)$ is, on average, $2\zeta(2)/n_i$ with $n_0 = 4$, $n_1 = n_2 = 2$. These are restated as Theorem 2.1 (Section 2.1) over the explicit real region with the $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$-invariant orbit count $N(V_{\mathbb{Z}}^{(i)}; X)$. The proof is Section 2's geometry of numbers: reduction theory for $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ acting on $V_{\mathbb{R}}$, the key technical difficulty being an unbounded cuspidal region (Davenport's averaging method, plus a proof that the cusp carries only reducible points so the main term is irreducible). A uniformity / squarefree-sieve estimate (Section 2.6, 2.7), embedding binary quartics into pairs of ternary quadratic forms, controls the error as the congruence conditions are sieved.

**Section 1.3 and 3.1 through 3.2: binary quartics as $2$-Selmer elements.** The bridge to elliptic curves is a classical interpretation (Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer, ref [9]): a $2$-Selmer element is a locally soluble $2$-covering, a genus-one curve $C/\mathbb{Q}$ with $\phi: C \to E$ (iso over $\mathbb{C}$) and $\theta: C \to E$ (degree-$4$, over $\mathbb{Q}$), a twist of multiplication-by-$2$. There are natural bijections
$$\{\text{soluble } 2\text{-coverings}\}/\sim \;\leftrightarrow\; E(\mathbb{Q})/2E(\mathbb{Q}), \qquad \{\text{locally soluble } 2\text{-coverings}\}/\sim \;\leftrightarrow\; S_2(E).$$
Each locally soluble $2$-covering yields a binary quartic form $f$ (a double cover $C \to \mathbb{P}^1$ ramified at $4$ points) well-defined up to $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Q})$-equivalence, with $y^2 = f(x)$ the $2$-covering and invariants $(I(f), J(f))$ matching $(A,B)$ up to bounded powers of $2$ and $3$. This is exactly the mechanism inside Cremona's `mwrank`. So counting $S_2(E_{A,B})$ over $A,B$ becomes counting integral binary quartics weighted appropriately, which is Theorem 2.1 plus the congruence/sieve refinement.

**Section 3.3 through 3.4: local masses and the change-of-measure constant.** The global count is rewritten as a product of local masses. For a large family $F$, the global density of the weighted set $S(F)$ of locally soluble forms factors as a product over places of local $p$-adic masses
$$M_p(V,F) = \int_{(I,J) \in \mathrm{Inv}_p(F)} \frac{\#(E^{I,J}(\mathbb{Q}_p)/2 E^{I,J}(\mathbb{Q}_p))}{\#E^{I,J}(\mathbb{Q}_p)[2]} \, dI\, dJ \tag{54}$$
(Proposition 3.9), where the integrand is the local Selmer ratio attached to the curve $E^{I,J}$. The change-of-variables from coefficient measure to invariant measure is a Jacobian computation (Proposition 3.10 over $\mathbb{C}$, Proposition 3.11 over $\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}, \mathbb{Z}_p$): a nonzero rational constant $\mathcal{J}$ with $|\mathcal{J}| = 1/27$ (computed prime by prime in Propositions 3.12, 3.13; the value $|\mathcal{J}|_3 = 27$ at $p = 3$ exactly cancels the $1/27$). Remark 3.14 notes nothing here is special to binary quartics: it works for any coregular representation $(G,W)$ with a freely generated invariant ring, finite generic stabilizer, and $\sum \deg(I_j) = \dim W$.

**Section 3.5 through 3.6: assembling the constant $3$.** Theorem 3.17 gives the count of curves of bounded height in a family $F$ as $N(F;X) = M_\infty(F;X)\prod_p M_p(F) + o(X^{5/6})$. The main average is Theorem 3.19:
$$\lim_{X\to\infty} \frac{\sum_{E \in F,\, H'(E) < X} (\#S_2(E) - 1)}{\sum_{E \in F,\, H'(E) < X} 1} = \mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{Z})\backslash \mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{R})) \cdot \frac{M_\infty(V,F;X)}{M_\infty(F;X)} \prod_p \left[\mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_p)) \frac{M_p(V,F)}{M_p(F)}\right]. \tag{71}$$
The local Selmer ratios are then evaluated. Lemma 3.20 gives $\#(E(\mathbb{Q}_p)/2E(\mathbb{Q}_p)) = \#E(\mathbb{Q}_p)[2]$ for $p \ne 2$ and $= 2 \cdot \#E(\mathbb{Q}_p)[2]$ for $p = 2$, so $M_p(V,F)/M_p(F) = 1$ for $p \ne 2$ and $= 2$ for $p = 2$ (equation (74)). The archimedean ratio is $M_\infty(V,F;X)/M_\infty(F;X) = 1/2$. Substituting,
$$\text{average of } (\#S_2(E) - 1) = \mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{Z})\backslash \mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{R})) \prod_p \mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_p)) = 2\zeta(2)\prod_p (1 - p^{-2}) = 2,$$
the Tamagawa number of $\mathrm{PGL}_2/\mathbb{Q}$. Hence the average of $\#S_2(E)$ is $\boxed{3}$, proving Theorems 3.1, 1.1, and 1.3. The "$3$" is therefore the Tamagawa number $2$ of $\mathrm{PGL}_2$ plus the trivial class, an interpretation that also matches the Poonen-Rains and Delaunay heuristics (Section 1.2 discussion).

## Project mapping

1. **Proven, exactly.** Average size of $S_2(E)$ over $E/\mathbb{Q}$ ordered by height is $3$ (Theorem 1.1), unconditionally and over $\mathbb{Q}$. Average $2$-Selmer rank $\le 1.5$, hence average Mordell-Weil rank $\le 1.5$ (Corollary 1.2). Robust to finitely-many-congruence subfamilies (Theorem 1.3). No GRH, no BSD, no Sha-finiteness assumption is used.

2. **The gap.** This is a population statement, an average over the infinite family. It certifies nothing for any individual curve: not the rank, not the rank equality $\mathrm{ord}_{s=1} L(E,s) = \mathrm{rank}\, E(\mathbb{Q})$, not finiteness of Sha, not the leading-coefficient formula. In particular it does not enter the open rank $\geq 2$ regime per curve; a positive (small) proportion of curves have rank $\ge 2$ and this paper says nothing about whether BSD holds for them.

3. **Exact regime.** Population / averages over all $E/\mathbb{Q}$, archimedean ordering by naive height. Not $p$-adic in the Iwasawa sense; the only $p$-adic content is the local masses $M_p$ that assemble into the global constant. It is orthogonal to the analytic-rank-$0$/$1$ vs $\geq 2$ per-curve axis: it averages across all ranks at once.

4. **Detector scorecard.**
   - **Detector 1 (parity-only): PASSES (does not trigger).** The result is about the size of $S_2(E)$, a genuine $2$-descent quantity, not the root number. It does not reduce to a parity statement. But note the converse caution: it also is not a rank-equality statement, so it cannot be mistaken for closing BSD.
   - **Detector 2 (Sha-finiteness assumed): PASSES (clean).** The argument never assumes $\#\mathrm{Sha}$ finite. $\mathrm{Sha}_E[2]$ appears only as the cokernel in (1); the count is of $S_2(E)$, which is always finite. This is exactly why the paper is unconditional. (Contrast Bhargava-Skinner-Zhang, which does invoke Gross-Zagier-Kolyvagin and Skinner-Urban downstream; that finiteness lives in the consumer, not here.)
   - **Detector 3 (function-field mirage): PASSES, and is informative.** The number-field proof has a genuine function-field cousin (de Jong, ref [20], average $3$-Selmer over $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$ bounded), and Theorem 1.1 is described as "a precise version of de Jong's theorem over $\mathbb{Q}$." But the method here does NOT import a geometric Frobenius: it is geometry of numbers (counting lattice points in $V_{\mathbb{Z}}$), which is intrinsically a number-field tool. So it is the rare case where a $\mathbb{Q}$-result and an $\mathbb{F}_q(C)$-result coexist by genuinely different mechanisms. The detector is satisfied: no surface, no Frobenius, no smuggled geometry.

5. **Control pair (rank $\le 1$ proven vs rank $\ge 2$ open).** This paper sits beside both arms of the control pair without resolving the open arm. It bounds the average, which is consistent with (and predicted by) the minimalist picture where rank $\geq 2$ is density zero, but that density-zero claim (Goldfeld) is a conjecture and is not proven here. The "one missing object" of the atlas (a rank-$\ge 2$ construction of independent points or an unconditional Sha bound) is untouched: averaging cannot produce it, by design.

6. **What it feeds.** Primarily the statistics architecture (atlas 5) and its consumer Bhargava-Skinner-Zhang. For the proof program it is a boundary marker, not an input: it tells Direction 01 ([`01_rank_two_object.md`](../research_directions/01_rank_two_object.md)) that the rank-$\ge 2$ curves are statistically rare but still must be handled one at a time, and it confirms that a population method, however sharp, lands on the wrong side of the per-curve wall. The orbit-counting / coregular-representation machinery (Remark 3.14) is the more reusable export: it is the template later extended to higher $n$-Selmer ($3$-, $4$-, $5$-Selmer) and to other families, which is where any future statistical leverage on Sha would have to originate. No current experiment in [`experiments/PLAN.md`](../../../experiments/PLAN.md) computes $2$-Selmer averages; the natural validation hook is a small numerical check that mean $\#S_2(E)$ trends to $3$ on a height-ordered sample, parallel to the Sato-Tate thread.
