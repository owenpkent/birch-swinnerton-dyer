# Reading note: J. E. Cremona, "Algorithms for Modular Elliptic Curves," 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press (1997).

> **Role in the BSD program.** This is the *computational substrate* of the whole project. It is the book that turns each side of strong BSD into a finite, machine-runnable computation: modular symbols compute the analytic side (the newform $f$, its sign $w$, the rational ratio $L(f,1)/\Omega(f)$, the periods $\Omega_E$, and the leading derivative $L^{(r)}(f,1)$); the elliptic-curve algorithms compute the algebraic side (torsion, a Mordell-Weil basis via point search plus height pairing, the regulator $\operatorname{Reg}(E)$, the Tamagawa numbers, the period lattice, and a two-descent that bounds the rank and $\operatorname{Sha}[2]$). The numerical invariants the rest of this repo treats as bundled ground truth (`experiments/_shared/curve_data.py`: $a_p$, conductor, rank, $L$-data, regulators) descend from exactly these algorithms and the Cremona tables they produced. Crucially, the book is honest about its own logical strength: its analytic-rank determination is *numerical*, not a proof of the rank equality, and where it needs the rank equality (to read off $L^{(r)}$, or to upgrade a lower bound on the rank to the true rank) it imports Gross-Zagier + Kolyvagin for analytic rank $\le 1$ and, for the handful of rank-2 curves, pins only the *algebraic* rank by exhibited points plus the two-descent upper bound while leaving the analytic-equals-algebraic *equality* itself a numerical observation. It proves no new BSD instance; it makes both sides *computable* so the conjecture can be *checked* curve by curve.

## One-line takeaway

For each rational newform $f$ of weight 2 and level $N$ (equivalently each modular elliptic curve $E_f/\mathbb{Q}$, now all $E/\mathbb{Q}$ by modularity), the book gives exact integer-arithmetic algorithms over $H^+(N) = H_1(X_0(N),\mathbb{R})^+$ to compute the analytic side of strong BSD ($w$, $L(f,1)/\Omega(f)$ exactly via formula (2.8.10), periods $\omega_1,\omega_2$ and $\Omega_E$, and $L^{(r)}(f,1)$ via formula (2.13.2)), and algorithms over $E(\mathbb{Q})$ to compute the algebraic side (canonical heights and regulator via Silverman's local-height decomposition, a Mordell-Weil basis by height-bounded search, and the rank plus $\#\operatorname{Sha}[2]$ via two-descent); the analytic rank $r$ is found numerically (and is genuinely *known* only because $r \le 2$ for all curves in the tables, with $r \le 1$ certified by Gross-Zagier-Kolyvagin and the 18 conjectured-rank-2 cases pinned to algebraic rank exactly 2 by exhibited independent points plus the unconditional two-descent upper bound), so the book *checks* strong BSD on thousands of curves but proves it on none. Note this pins the algebraic rank; the *analytic* rank 2 of those curves remains a numerical observation, not a theorem, so even the BSD *rank equality* is only checked, never proved, in the rank-2 cases.

## Technical content (section by section)

### Chapter II $\S 2.1$-$\S 2.4$: modular symbols, M-symbols, and the Hecke action (pp. 7-22)

**Homology as the computable object.** The method computes $H_1(X_0(N),\mathbb{Q})$ explicitly and exploits the duality (2.1.3) $S_2(\Gamma_0(N)) \times H_1(X_0(N),\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{C}$, $(f,\gamma) \mapsto \langle\gamma,f\rangle = \int_\gamma 2\pi i f(z)\,dz$, an exact $\mathbb{C}$-duality between cusp forms and homology (p. 10). The integral $\langle\gamma,f\rangle$ is a *period* of $f$.

**Modular symbols** $\{\alpha,\beta\}_G \in H_1(X_G,\mathbb{Q})$ for $\alpha,\beta \in \mathcal{H}^*$ satisfy the formalism of Proposition 2.1.1 (p. 12): $\{\alpha,\alpha\}=0$, $\{\alpha,\beta\}+\{\beta,\alpha\}=0$, the 3-term relation $\{\alpha,\beta\}+\{\beta,\gamma\}+\{\gamma,\alpha\}=0$, $\Gamma_0(N)$-invariance $\{M\alpha,M\beta\}=\{\alpha,\beta\}$, and $\{\alpha,M\alpha\}\in H_1(X_G,\mathbb{Z})$. **Theorem 2.1.3 (Manin-Drinfeld, p. 13):** for $G$ a congruence subgroup and cusps $\alpha,\beta$, $\{\alpha,\beta\}_G \in H_1(X_G,\mathbb{Q})$; in particular $\{0,\infty\}$ is rational, and its denominator is "very important in many ways."

**Real structure.** For $G$ of real type the involution $*$ (from $z \mapsto -\bar z$) splits $H_1(X_G,\mathbb{R}) = H_1^+ \oplus H_1^-$ with $\dim H_1^\pm = g$ (p. 11), and the duality restricts to the *real* pairing $S_2(G)_\mathbb{R} \times H_1^+(X_G,\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$ (2.1.4). Working in $H^+(N)$ halves the dimension and is the substrate for the whole first phase.

**M-symbols (Manin) and $\Gamma_0(N)$.** Cosets $\Gamma_0(N)\backslash\Gamma \leftrightarrow P^1(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})$ via M-symbols $(c:d)$ (p. 15-16), with the relation $(c:d)\sim(c':d') \iff cd'\equiv c'd \pmod N$. Generators satisfy the 2-term relation $(c:d)+(-d:c)=0$ (2.2.6) and the 3-term relation $(c:d)+(c+d:-c)+(d:-c-d)=0$ (2.2.7); $H(N)$ is the kernel of the boundary map $\delta:(c:d)\mapsto[a/c]-[b/d]$, computed by integer Gaussian elimination, giving $\dim H(N)=2g$. This is the *entire homology computation*, "we do not have to do any geometry at all" (p. 14).

**Hecke action (2.4.1, p. 18):** for $p\nmid N$, $T_p\{\alpha,\beta\} = \{p\alpha,p\beta\} + \sum_{r \bmod p}\{(\alpha+r)/p,(\beta+r)/p\}$, inducing the operator on $H(N)$ whose rational integer eigenvalues are the $a_p$ of newforms.

### Chapter II $\S 2.6$-$\S 2.7$: newforms and the splitting (pp. 24-28)

A **rational newform** $f = \sum a(n,f)q^n$ is a simultaneous Hecke eigenform with $a(n,f)\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $a(1,f)=1$; for $p\nmid N$, $a(p,f)=a_p$, and the Fricke involution $W_N$ gives the sign $w = -\varepsilon_N$ of the functional equation (p. 28). The newform attaches an elliptic curve $E_f = \mathbb{C}/\Lambda_f$ of conductor $N$ with $L(E_f,s) = L(f,s)$ (Wiles-BCDT modularity makes every $E/\mathbb{Q}$ of this form). Eigenspaces are split off recursively in $H^+(N)$ by diagonalizing the $W_q$ and $T_p$, discarding oldclasses via the multiplicity formula (2.7.1). Output of phase 1 (the **recapitulation**, p. 32): the number of newforms, the sign $w$, the *exact* ratio $L(f,1)/\Omega(f)$, all $\varepsilon_q$, and many $a_p$.

### Chapter II $\S 2.8$: $L(f,s)$ and the exact evaluation of $L(f,1)/\Omega(f)$ (pp. 29-31)

$L(f,s) = (2\pi)^s\Gamma(s)^{-1}\int_0^{i\infty}(-iz)^s f(z)\,dz/z = \sum a(n,f)n^{-s}$ (2.8.1-2.8.2), with Euler product (2.8.3) and the functional equation $\Lambda(f,2-s) = -\varepsilon_N\Lambda(f,s)$ for $\Lambda(f,s)=N^{s/2}(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(f,s)$ (2.8.6). At the center, $L(f,1) = -\langle\{0,\infty\},f\rangle$ (2.8.7), so $L(f,1)$ is a *rational* multiple of a real period. The "closing the path" trick with $(1+p-T_p)\{0,\infty\} = \sum_{k}\{0,k/p\}$ (2.8.8) yields the central formula

$$\frac{L(f,1)}{\Omega(f)} = \frac{n(p,f)}{2(1+p-a_p)} \tag{2.8.10}$$

with $n(p,f)\in\mathbb{Z}$ (the integral projection of the right side onto the $f$-eigenspace) and $\Omega(f)$ the least real period, doubled when the lattice is rectangular: $\Omega(f) = 2\Omega_0(f)$ (rectangular) or $\Omega_0(f)$ (otherwise), so $\Omega(f)/\Omega_0(f)$ is the number of real components of $E_f$. **This is the analytic side of strong BSD, computed exactly as a rational number.** $L(f,1)=0 \iff n(p,f)=0 \iff E_f(\mathbb{Q})$ conjecturally infinite. The book records this ratio for every newform and observes it is "consistent with the Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture in each case" (p. 31), i.e. it *checks*, it does not prove.

### Chapter II $\S 2.10$, $\S 2.14$, $\S 3.7$: periods and the curve equation (pp. 33-36, 45, 97-98)

The full period lattice $\Lambda_f = \mathbb{Z}\omega_1 + \mathbb{Z}\omega_2$ is computed in $H(N)$ by writing each generating period $\langle\gamma_j,f\rangle = a_j x + b_j y i$ from the $\pm$-eigenvectors $v^\pm$, with $x = \langle\gamma^+,f\rangle$, $y = -i\langle\gamma^-,f\rangle$ real (2.10.1-2.10.2), and reducing the lattice of $(a_j,b_j)$ to a basis. The "Type 1 / Type 2" dichotomy distinguishes negative-discriminant ($\Omega(f)=\Omega_0(f)$) from rectangular positive-discriminant ($\Omega(f)=2\Omega_0(f)$) lattices. Periods are summed from the rapidly convergent series (2.10.5)/(2.10.8) in $\exp(-2\pi/d\sqrt N)$. From $\tau=\omega_1/\omega_2$ one gets $c_4,c_6$ via (2.14.1) and, using Edixhoven's integrality plus conductor congruences, the exact minimal Weierstrass equation. Section 3.7 gives the inverse direction (curve $\to$ periods) by Gauss's AGM: $\lambda_1 = \pi/\mathrm{AGM}(\sqrt{e_3-e_1},\sqrt{e_3-e_2})$ (3.7.1), used as an independent cross-check.

### Chapter II $\S 2.13$: computing $L^{(r)}(f,1)$, the leading term (pp. 42-44)

For analytic rank $r$ (with sign forcing $\varepsilon = (-1)^{r-1}$), the leading derivative is

$$L^{(r)}(f,1) = 2\, r! \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{a(n,f)}{n}\, G_r\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{\sqrt N}\right), \qquad G_r(x) = \frac{1}{(r-1)!}\int_1^\infty e^{-xy}(\log y)^{r-1}\frac{dy}{y} \tag{2.13.2}$$

(Proposition 2.13.1), with $G_r(x) = P_r(\log(1/x)) + \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n-r}}{n^r\, n!}x^n$ and explicit $P_r = Q_r(t-\gamma)$ ($\gamma$ = Euler's constant), e.g. $Q_2(t) = \tfrac12 t^2 + \tfrac{\pi^2}{12}$, $Q_3(t) = \tfrac16 t^3 + \tfrac{\pi^2}{12}t - \tfrac{\zeta(3)}{3}$ (p. 44). **The honest limitation, stated in the book (p. 44):** "no approximate calculation can by itself determine" whether $L^{(k)}(f,1)=0$. For $N<5077$ all curves have $r\le2$, so $r$ is read off cheaply ($r=0\iff L(f,1)\ne0$ via the exact (2.8.10); $r=1$ by checking $L'(f,1)\ne0$ numerically when $\varepsilon=+1$ and $L(f,1)=0$; $r=2$ by checking $L''(f,1)\ne0$ when $\varepsilon=-1$). For $N=5077$ (rank 3, the Buhler-Gross-Zagier curve) the book is explicit: one computes $L'(f,1)=0$ to 13 places, then *invokes the Gross-Zagier theorem* to conclude $L'(f,1)=0$ exactly (a Heegner point of small positive height forces it), so $r\ge3$; numerically $L^{(3)}(f,1)\approx1.73\ne0$ gives $r=3$. **This is the structural ceiling:** the analytic rank above 1 is pinned only by combining an approximation with an external rank-$\le1$ theorem, not by the modular-symbol machine alone.

### Chapter III $\S 3.4$: heights, the height pairing, and the regulator (pp. 71-74)

The canonical height $\hat h(P)$ is computed via Silverman's decomposition $\hat h(P) = \sum_{p\le\infty}\hat h_p(P)$ (3.4.1), using the **BSD-normalized** height (twice Silverman's, so the regulator is $2^r$ times larger; footnote: "the two sides of the Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture disagreed by a factor of $2^r$!" until normalized). Local heights at finite $p$ are the case analysis of **Proposition 3.4.1** (good/multiplicative/additive reduction giving rational multiples of $\log p$); the archimedean local height $\hat h_\infty(P) = \log|x| + \tfrac14\sum_{n\ge0}4^{-n}c_n$ is Tate's series as amended by Silverman, with an explicit truncation bound for $d$-digit accuracy. The **regulator** is

$$\operatorname{Reg}(E) = \big|\det(\hat h(P_i,P_j))\big|, \qquad \hat h(P,Q) = \tfrac12(\hat h(P+Q)-\hat h(P)-\hat h(Q)).$$

This is the regulator factor of strong BSD, computed to arbitrary precision.

### Chapter III $\S 3.5$-$\S 3.6$: Mordell-Weil generators, the rank, and $\operatorname{Sha}[2]$ (pp. 75-97)

**Generators ($\S 3.5$).** Points of infinite order are found by height-bounded search (quadratic sieve over denominators $c$, naive height $h(P)=\log\max\{|a|,c^2\}$); the height pairing decides independence (nonzero determinant), index gain ($kP$ relation), and basis enlargement. Silverman's bound (Proposition 3.5.1) $-\tfrac1{12}h(j)-\mu(E)-1.922 \le \hat h(P)-h(P) \le \mu(E)+2.14$ converts a known rank into a finite search certifying a *full* $\mathbb{Z}$-basis (Lemma 3.5.2/Zagier: $\{P:\hat h(P)\le B\}$ generates if it covers $E(\mathbb{Q})/2E(\mathbb{Q})$).

**The rank ($\S 3.6$).** The rank is "by far the hardest of the elementary quantities." The method is **two-descent** (general, or via 2-isogeny when there is a rational 2-torsion point): an injection $E(\mathbb{Q})/2E(\mathbb{Q}) \hookrightarrow$ 2-Selmer group, realized by classifying principal homogeneous spaces (quartics $y^2 = g(x)$ of degree 4) and testing them for local solubility (explicit $\mathbb{Q}_p$- and $\mathbb{R}$-algorithms, `lemma6`/`lemma7`) and global solubility (point search only). **The strictly honest statement (p. 78, p. 83, p. 97):** two-descent is "not an algorithm" because deciding whether a locally-soluble quartic has a *global* rational point is not known to be decidable; quartics that are everywhere locally soluble but have no global point are exactly the nontrivial elements of $\operatorname{Sha}(E)[2]$, so in their presence one obtains only **bounds** $\tilde e_1 - e_0 \le \operatorname{rank} E(\mathbb{Q}) \le e_2 - e_0$ and $|\operatorname{Sha}(E/\mathbb{Q})[2]| \le n_2/\tilde n_1$ (p. 97). The Selmer-rank upper bound $e_2 - e_0$ is unconditional; the lower bound improves to the exact rank only when independent points are actually exhibited, and the Selmer/Sha-finiteness conjecture is what would close the residual gap in general (it predicts $n_2/n_1$ is a perfect square, $e_2 \equiv \tilde e_1 \bmod 2$). **For the tables:** Kolyvagin and Gross-Zagier already settled all rank-0 and rank-1 cases (p. 44, p. 78); "just 18 cases of conjectured rank 2" remained, and for these the rank was pinned to exactly 2 by the *unconditional* combination of two independent exhibited points (rank $\ge 2$) with the descent upper bound (rank $\le 2$), with no appeal to Sha-finiteness for the rank itself (p. 78). $\operatorname{Reg}$, $\operatorname{Sha}[2]$, and a basis of $E(\mathbb{Q})/2E(\mathbb{Q})$ fall out of the same computation.

### Chapter III $\S 3.8$: isogenies, and the assembled BSD data (pp. 98-103, Table 4)

Isogenous curves are found by Velu's formulae after detecting rational $l$-isogenies (semistable: $l\in\{2,3,5,7\}$ by Serre; otherwise the finite Mazur list $\{11,17,19,37,43,67,163\}$ keyed to $j$-invariants). Tamagawa numbers $c_p$ come from Tate's algorithm ($\S 3.2$). The combination of all of the above is precisely the strong-BSD data printed in **Table 4** (BSD data, p. 313 ff.): for each curve the columns realize

$$\frac{L^{(r)}(E,1)}{r!} \;\overset{?}{=}\; \frac{\Omega_E \cdot \operatorname{Reg}(E)\cdot \prod_p c_p \cdot \#\operatorname{Sha}(E)}{(\#E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}})^2},$$

with every factor on the right computed exactly or to high precision *except* $\#\operatorname{Sha}$, which is *back-solved* from the identity (i.e. the analytic side divided by the computed period/regulator/Tamagawa/torsion), and then observed to be a perfect square. **This is the inversion that defines the whole approach: $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ is read off assuming BSD, not proven.**

## Project mapping

1. **What is PROVEN here vs the GAP.** PROVEN: nothing new about BSD. The book proves *correctness of algorithms* (e.g. Manin-Drinfeld Theorem 2.1.3, the homology isomorphism Theorem 2.1.4, the period formulas 2.8.10/2.10.8/2.13.2, Silverman's height decomposition 3.4.1) and *computes* both sides of strong BSD for thousands of curves. GAP: every BSD *equality* it touches is either (a) a numerical check, or (b) imported as a theorem for rank $\le 1$ (Gross-Zagier-Kolyvagin), or (c) a two-descent *verification* in the 18 rank-2 cases that is conditional on the rank being correctly bounded and on Sha-finiteness for the exact value. It upgrades no conjecture.

2. **EXACT regime.** **Archimedean and computational**, all ranks present but only $r\le2$ (later $r\le3$+) actually appearing in the range computed; the analytic-rank determination is rigorous *only* for $r\le1$ (via the imported Kolyvagin/Gross-Zagier theorems). For $r=2$ the analytic rank stays a *numerical observation* (one sees $L(f,1)=L'(f,1)=0$ and $L''(f,1)\ne0$ only to finite precision, with no external theorem certifying $L'(f,1)=0$ exactly, unlike the $r=3$ curve 5077 where Gross-Zagier supplies that step); what is pinned unconditionally there is the *algebraic* rank, via exhibited points plus the descent upper bound. The $p$-adic side is absent here (that is the Mazur-Swinnerton-Dyer / MTT story); this book is the real-analytic and integer-arithmetic instrument. So on the project's central line it lives entirely on the **proven rank-$\le1$ side**, with a thin extension to the 18 rank-2 curves that pins their *algebraic* rank but not the BSD rank equality, and constructs **no rank-$\ge2$ object**.

3. **Detector 1 (parity-only): PASSES.** The book computes the full order $r$ and the actual leading term $L^{(r)}(f,1)$, not merely $w$ (which it also computes as $-\varepsilon_N$). The sign is used only to know the parity of $r$ before pinning $r$ exactly. It is the opposite of a parity-only method: it measures the leading coefficient. (The honest caveat it states itself: above $r=1$ the measurement is an approximation that must be closed by an external theorem.)

4. **Detector 2 (Sha-finiteness assumed): TRIGGERED, and this is the canonical example.** Two-descent yields only $\operatorname{Sha}(E)[2]$ and rank *bounds* (p. 97); the rank can be pinned unconditionally when independent points happen to be exhibited (as for the 18 rank-2 curves), but closing the bound *in general*, and reading off the full $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ (not just its 2-part), requires the Sha-finiteness conjecture (perfect-square order, $n_2/n_1$ a square). The Table-4 value of $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ is *back-solved from BSD itself*, so it is a BSD-conditional output, not an unconditional computation, in every rank. In the open rank-$\ge2$ regime the assumption is load-bearing. The detector flags this: any experiment that ingests Cremona's $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ as ground truth is ingesting a BSD-conditional number.

5. **Detector 3 (function-field mirage): PASSES cleanly.** Everything is genuinely over $\mathbb{Q}$: modular symbols on $X_0(N)/\mathbb{Q}$, real/complex periods, $p$-adic *local* solubility tests over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ and $\mathbb{R}$ only as components of a global descent. No geometric Frobenius over a finite field is imported; the difficulty (global solubility of quartics, finiteness of Sha) is faced head-on and left open exactly where the number-field case is open.

6. **Control pair (rank $\le1$ proven vs rank $\ge2$ open).** This book *is* the instrument that populates the control pair with data: it computes `37a1` (rank 1), `389a1` (rank 2), `5077a1` (rank 3). It demonstrates the wall concretely: for `37a1` and all rank-1 curves the analytic rank is certified by Kolyvagin; for `389a1` and the 17 other rank-2 curves it is only *verified* by two-descent plus exhibited points, never *proven equal* to the analytic rank by the modular machine; for `5077a1` even establishing $r\ge3$ requires invoking Gross-Zagier to turn a numerical $L'=0$ into an exact one. The book is therefore the cleanest empirical witness that the Heegner/modular-symbol pipeline produces *one* point and an *approximate* higher derivative, and so structurally cannot certify rank $\ge2$ on its own.

7. **Which direction / experiment it feeds.** Primary feed: **Research Direction 01** ([`01_rank_two_object.md`](../research_directions/01_rank_two_object.md)) - it is the empirical demonstration of why the proven regime stops at rank 1 and what an unconditional rank-2 certificate would have to replace (the two-descent's global-solubility oracle and the back-solved $\#\operatorname{Sha}$). Secondary feeds: **Direction 03** (it computes the archimedean leading term $L^{(r)}(f,1)$ and the period $\Omega_E$ that the $p$-adic-to-archimedean bridge must match) and **Direction 02** (it shows precisely where the Euler-system theorem is consumed as a black box). Experiments: this is the *source of the bundled invariants* in [`experiments/_shared/curve_data.py`](../../../experiments/_shared/curve_data.py) (Cremona labels, $a_p$, conductor, rank, $L$-data, regulators), and the algorithmic reference for [`experiments/l_function_rank/`](../../../experiments/) (the AFE evaluation of $L(E,1)$ and $L'(E,1)$ mirrors formulas 2.8.10 and 2.13.2) and [`experiments/strong_bsd_quantities/`](../../../experiments/) (the strong-BSD identity assembled in $\S 3.4$-$\S 3.8$ and Table 4). The single most important methodological warning it supplies to the experimental thread: **Cremona's $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ is computed *assuming* BSD, so an experiment must never treat it as an independent confirmation of BSD.**
