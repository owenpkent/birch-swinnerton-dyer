# Reading note: J. W. S. Cassels, "Diophantine equations with special reference to elliptic curves," Journal of the London Mathematical Society s1-41 (1966), 193-291

> **Role in the BSD program.** This is the survey that fixed the modern vocabulary of the arithmetic side of BSD: descent via the $n$-descent exact sequence, the Selmer group as the computable upper bound on the rank, the Tate-Shafarevich group $\operatorname{Sha}$ (Cassels' own notation, the Russian "Ш") as the obstruction measuring the failure of the Hasse principle on principal homogeneous spaces, and the Cassels-Tate pairing as a non-degenerate (when $\operatorname{Sha}$ is finite) alternating pairing on $\operatorname{Sha}$. It states the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer on the arithmetic side, with $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ appearing in the leading-coefficient formula, and it is the origin of the structural fact that $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ is a perfect square when finite (for an elliptic curve). It is the foundational reference that every later note in this folder silently presupposes for the definition of the objects ($\operatorname{Sel}$, $\operatorname{Sha}$, the regulator, the local Tamagawa data).

## One-line takeaway

Cassels' survey is where descent, the Selmer group, the Tate-Shafarevich group $\operatorname{Sha}$, and the Cassels-Tate pairing acquire their modern form: descent realizes the rank-bounding exact sequence $0 \to E(\mathbb{Q})/nE(\mathbb{Q}) \to \operatorname{Sel}^{(n)}(E) \to \operatorname{Sha}(E)[n] \to 0$; $\operatorname{Sha}$ is the group of everywhere-locally-trivial torsors, the precise obstruction between the computable Selmer bound and the actual rank; and the alternating Cassels-Tate pairing forces $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ to be a perfect square *when finite*. That last clause is the load-bearing one for this project: the pairing is a *constraint on the shape* of $\operatorname{Sha}$, not a proof that $\operatorname{Sha}$ is finite. Finiteness of $\operatorname{Sha}$ remains open in general and is a theorem only in analytic rank $\leq 1$ (Kolyvagin), so the square-order result is a structural fact one may invoke only after finiteness has been supplied from outside.

## Technical content (section by section)

The survey is long and expository (pp. 193-291), organized as a tour of the descent apparatus for elliptic and higher-genus curves over number fields, building up to the Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture on the arithmetic side. The logical spine relevant to this project has five movements: (A) the group law and the Mordell-Weil theorem as the thing to be made effective; (B) descent and the Selmer group; (C) principal homogeneous spaces and the definition of $\operatorname{Sha}$; (D) the Cassels-Tate pairing and the square-order consequence; (E) the statement of BSD on the arithmetic side.

**(A) The Mordell-Weil group and the descent problem.** The starting point is the Mordell-Weil theorem: for $E$ over a number field $K$, the group $E(K)$ of rational points is finitely generated, $E(K) \cong E(K)_{\mathrm{tors}} \oplus \mathbb{Z}^r$. The proof has two halves: the *weak Mordell-Weil theorem*, that $E(K)/nE(K)$ is finite, and the theory of heights, that lets one promote a basis of $E(K)/nE(K)$ to generators of $E(K)$ via the descent / infinite-descent argument. The survey's central methodological point is that the weak Mordell-Weil theorem is *not effective*: the proof bounds $E(K)/nE(K)$ inside a finite computable group but does not by itself produce generators or the rank $r$. Making it effective is the descent problem, and the obstruction to effectivity is exactly $\operatorname{Sha}$.

**(B) Descent and the Selmer group.** Fix an integer $n \geq 2$ (Cassels treats general $n$ and isogeny descent, not only multiplication-by-$n$). The Kummer sequence $0 \to E[n] \to E \xrightarrow{n} E \to 0$ of $G_K$-modules gives, in Galois cohomology, the short exact sequence
$$0 \to E(K)/nE(K) \xrightarrow{\delta} H^1(K, E[n]) \to H^1(K,E)[n] \to 0.$$
The class group / unit obstructions make $H^1(K, E[n])$ itself too large to be directly finite, so one cuts it down by local conditions. The **Selmer group** is
$$\operatorname{Sel}^{(n)}(E/K) = \ker\Big( H^1(K, E[n]) \to \prod_{v} H^1(K_v, E) \Big),$$
the classes that are *everywhere locally in the image of the local Kummer map* (i.e. become trivial in $H^1(K_v, E)$ for every place $v$). The Selmer group is finite and effectively computable (descent computes it). It sits in the fundamental descent exact sequence
$$0 \to E(K)/nE(K) \to \operatorname{Sel}^{(n)}(E/K) \to \operatorname{Sha}(E/K)[n] \to 0,$$
which is the precise statement that the Selmer group is the *computable upper bound* on $E(K)/nE(K)$, and the discrepancy is the $n$-torsion of $\operatorname{Sha}$.

**(C) Principal homogeneous spaces and the definition of $\operatorname{Sha}$ (the subgroup of the Weil-Chatelet group cut out by everywhere-local triviality, denoted by the Russian "Ш").** Cassels gives $H^1(K, E)$ its geometric meaning: this whole group is the **Weil-Chatelet group** $\mathrm{WC}(E/K)$, classifying *principal homogeneous spaces* (torsors) under $E$, i.e. genus-$1$ curves $C/K$ with a simply transitive $E$-action, up to isomorphism. The trivial class is the curves with a $K$-rational point ($C \cong E$). $\operatorname{Sha}$ is then the *subgroup* of the Weil-Chatelet group consisting of the everywhere-locally-trivial torsors, not the whole group: the two must not be conflated. The **Tate-Shafarevich group** is the subgroup of torsors that have a point *everywhere locally* but possibly not globally:
$$\operatorname{Sha}(E/K) = \ker\Big( H^1(K, E) \to \prod_v H^1(K_v, E) \Big).$$
Cassels' notation for this group is the Cyrillic letter **Ш** (Russian "Sha," for Shafarevich), and that notation enters the literature here. The arithmetic meaning: $\operatorname{Sha}$ measures the failure of the *Hasse / local-global principle* for genus-$1$ curves under $E$. A nonzero element is a curve that is a counterexample to the Hasse principle: solutions in every completion $K_v$, no solution in $K$. The conjecture that $\operatorname{Sha}$ is *finite* is stated as a conjecture (it is open in general to this day, and known only in analytic rank $\leq 1$).

**(D) The Cassels-Tate pairing and the square-order consequence.** Cassels constructs a pairing
$$\langle\,,\,\rangle : \operatorname{Sha}(E/K) \times \operatorname{Sha}(E/K) \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$$
(for an elliptic curve, using the principal polarization to identify $E$ with its dual $\hat E$; Tate generalized it to $\operatorname{Sha}(A) \times \operatorname{Sha}(\hat A) \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ for abelian varieties $A$). The key properties Cassels establishes:
> - The pairing is **bilinear** and its kernel is exactly the divisible part of $\operatorname{Sha}$. Hence **if $\operatorname{Sha}$ is finite, the pairing is non-degenerate.**
> - For an elliptic curve the pairing is **alternating**: $\langle x, x \rangle = 0$ for all $x$.

A finite abelian group carrying a non-degenerate *alternating* bilinear pairing into $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ has square order (it is a symplectic-type module: it decomposes into hyperbolic pieces). Therefore:
$$\boxed{\text{If } \operatorname{Sha}(E/K) \text{ is finite, then } \#\operatorname{Sha}(E/K) \text{ is a perfect square.}}$$
This is the result the present project cares about most. Two cautions that the survey's logic enforces and that this note will not let slip:
1. The square-order statement is **conditional on finiteness**. The pairing is non-degenerate only modulo the divisible subgroup; if $\operatorname{Sha}$ had an infinite divisible part the conclusion would be vacuous. The theorem is "finite $\Rightarrow$ square," never "$\Rightarrow$ finite."
2. The "alternating" property is what gives *square* (rather than merely "a square times a possible factor of $2$"). For elliptic curves the pairing is genuinely alternating and the order is a square. (For Jacobians of higher-genus curves the later refinement of Poonen-Stoll shows the order can be twice a square when there is no rational point of odd degree; that subtlety is beyond Cassels 1966 and is noted here only so the elliptic-curve statement is not over-generalized.)

**(E) The arithmetic side of the Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture.** The survey states BSD as it stood in 1966, emphasizing the arithmetic invariants that appear in the conjectural leading coefficient. In modern normalization the strong form predicts, for $E/\mathbb{Q}$ of rank $r$,
$$\lim_{s \to 1} \frac{L(E,s)}{(s-1)^r} = \frac{\#\operatorname{Sha}(E/\mathbb{Q}) \cdot \Omega_E \cdot \operatorname{Reg}_E \cdot \prod_p c_p}{\#E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}^2},$$
with $\Omega_E$ the real period, $\operatorname{Reg}_E$ the regulator of the height pairing on $E(\mathbb{Q})$, $c_p$ the local Tamagawa numbers, and $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ the order of the (conjecturally finite) Tate-Shafarevich group. Cassels' contribution at this point is the *arithmetic* assembly: the rank as the rank of $E(K)$, the role of $\operatorname{Sha}$ as the term that makes the formula sensitive to the local-global failure, and the regulator from the canonical height pairing. The analytic existence of $L(E,s)$ at $s=1$ (continuation, functional equation) is *not* a theorem in 1966; the survey treats the $L$-function side conjecturally. This is the correct place to record that the parity/root-number machinery this project tracks under modularity is a *later* development and is not part of Cassels' contribution.

## Project mapping

1. **What is PROVEN here.** (a) The descent exact sequence $0 \to E(K)/nE(K) \to \operatorname{Sel}^{(n)} \to \operatorname{Sha}[n] \to 0$, with $\operatorname{Sel}^{(n)}$ finite and effectively computable. (b) The interpretation of $H^1(K,E)$ as torsors and of $\operatorname{Sha}$ as the everywhere-locally-trivial torsors (the local-global obstruction). (c) The Cassels-Tate pairing, bilinear with kernel the divisible part, alternating for elliptic curves, hence: **$\operatorname{Sha}$ finite $\Rightarrow \#\operatorname{Sha}$ a perfect square.** All three are theorems, stated as such.

2. **What is the GAP / what is NOT proven here.** (i) **Finiteness of $\operatorname{Sha}$ is conjectural** in this survey and remains open in general today; it is a theorem only in analytic rank $\leq 1$ (Kolyvagin), which is *later* work, not Cassels'. (ii) Descent is only effective *given* a way to decide whether each locally-trivial Selmer class is globally trivial, which is precisely deciding membership in $\operatorname{Sha}$: the survey makes the obstruction explicit but does not remove it. (iii) The square-order result computes nothing about the rank: a square $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ is consistent with every rank, so this is structure on the *error term*, not on the *main term* (the rank). (iv) The entire analytic side of BSD (continuation of $L$, functional equation, root number) is conjectural in 1966.

3. **EXACT regime.** Regime-neutral on rank: the descent apparatus and the definition of $\operatorname{Sha}$ are stated for $E$ of *any* rank over any number field, with no rank-$\leq 1$ restriction. This is what makes the survey foundational rather than rank-bounded: it furnishes the objects that the rank-$\geq 2$ open problem is *about*. The content is **arithmetic / global**, not $p$-adic and not archimedean in the analytic sense; the only archimedean ingredient that surfaces is the real period $\Omega_E$ in the BSD leading-term formula (E), and the height/regulator pairing. No analytic-rank hypothesis is used anywhere because no $L$-function analysis is performed.

4. **Detector scores.**
   - **Detector 1 (parity-only): PASSES (not applicable).** This survey contains no parity argument and no root-number computation: it predates modularity-as-theorem and does not touch the analytic continuation needed to define the root number. It supplies the $\operatorname{Sha}$ in which parity phenomena (e.g. the parity conjecture, the square-order constraint) are later expressed, but it makes no rank-mod-$2$ claim itself. Nothing here masquerades as full rank equality.
   - **Detector 2 (Sha-finiteness assumed): THIS IS THE SOURCE OF THE DETECTOR'S OBJECT, and it is scrupulously honest about the hypothesis.** The square-order theorem is explicitly conditional: "if $\operatorname{Sha}$ is finite." The survey states finiteness as a *conjecture*. The detector exists precisely to catch later arguments that quietly use "$\#\operatorname{Sha}$ is a square" or "the pairing is non-degenerate" as if finiteness were free. Cassels does not commit that error; any note or experiment that invokes the square-order fact must carry the finiteness flag, which is known only for analytic rank $\leq 1$. **The pairing is a constraint on shape, not a proof of finiteness.**
   - **Detector 3 (function-field mirage): PASSES, with a sharp lesson.** Cassels works over number fields; $\operatorname{Sha}$ and the pairing are defined Galois-cohomologically with no geometric Frobenius. The mirage is the *contrast*: over a function field $\mathbb{F}_q(C)$, the Tate / Artin-Tate theorems show that BSD for the associated elliptic surface, finiteness of $\operatorname{Sha}$ (equivalently of the Brauer group of the surface), and the Tate conjecture for that surface are *equivalent*, so that BSD holds there once $\operatorname{Sha}$ is known finite (and finiteness is itself accessible in many cases via the geometric Frobenius and the proven instances of the Tate conjecture). The decisive extra ingredient is the geometric Frobenius acting on $\ell$-adic cohomology of the surface, which the number-field setting lacks entirely. Cassels' number-field $\operatorname{Sha}$ has *no* such Frobenius, which is exactly why finiteness stays open here. This survey is the clean number-field baseline against which the function-field mirage is recognized.

5. **Control pair (rank $\leq 1$ proven vs rank $\geq 2$ open).** This note sits *underneath* the control pair rather than on one side of it. The descent sequence and $\operatorname{Sha}$ are defined identically in both regimes; the rank-$\leq 1$ proven results (Gross-Zagier + Kolyvagin) are theorems about *this same* $\operatorname{Sha}$ and *this same* Selmer group, supplying from Heegner-point Euler systems the finiteness that Cassels could only conjecture. The rank-$\geq 2$ open problem is the problem of producing, by some new mechanism, the finiteness and the rank lower bound for *these very objects*. So Cassels 1966 is the shared substrate of the control pair: it defines the ruler, not the measurement.

6. **Which direction / experiment it feeds.** Primary: Research Direction 01 ([`01_rank_two_object.md`](../research_directions/01_rank_two_object.md)). The rank-$\geq 2$ object a proof must construct lives *inside* the Selmer/$\operatorname{Sha}$ framework set up here; any candidate construction (a second-order Euler system, a higher Heegner-type cycle) is judged by what it does to $\operatorname{Sel}^{(n)}$ and $\operatorname{Sha}[n]$ in this exact sequence. Secondary: Direction 02 ([`02_higher_rank_euler_system.md`](../research_directions/02_higher_rank_euler_system.md)), since an Euler system is precisely a tool for bounding the Selmer group defined here. Experiments: the descent exact sequence and the $\#\operatorname{Sha}$ term in the strong-BSD formula are what `experiments/strong_bsd_quantities` ($\#\operatorname{Sha}$, $\operatorname{Reg}_E$, $\Omega_E$, $c_p$) and `experiments/weak_bsd_table` (rank vs Selmer rank) are computing against; the square-order constraint is a built-in sanity check on any computed $\#\operatorname{Sha}$.
